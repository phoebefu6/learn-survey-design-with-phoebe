Learn Survey Design & Sampling with Phoebe · Leader session 3 of 4

What the number can carry: the decision sets the error budget

A survey number is never just right or wrong; it is close enough or not close enough for a particular decision. The same 20.2% ± 5.5 that is plenty for a newsletter can be useless for a vote that turns on 20%. Tonight you work out what Ottervale's resident survey can carry for the council's bus and library call, which of its numbers should never be printed, and when the council should not have run a survey at all. Ottervale is a constructed town; the lab underneath it is real census rows.

Part 0

Where we start

Last session you bought a survey with a brief and a methods statement. Tonight the results are back and the council wants to act on them. Three moves: start from the decision and ask how wrong the number can be before the decision would flip; find the cells too thin to report, before anyone quotes them; and recognise the situations where a survey, however well run, is the wrong instrument. The survey error lab supplies the evidence: Ottervale's question asked of 20,000 real census adults, so you can see what each number can and cannot carry against a known truth.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A one-page decision memo for Ottervale's council: the decision, the error it can tolerate, what the survey can and cannot carry, the cells not to report, and the condition under which the council should not use the survey at all. It is the page that should sit on top of any survey result before a vote.

Part 1 · covers the decision and the error it can tolerate

Start from the call, then ask how wrong is too wrong

5 min live

Ottervale's council has a rule: keep the bus route if at least 20% of adults would be cut off without it. That threshold is constructed, and on the lab the census health measure stands in for the bus question. The survey comes back at 20.2% ± 5.5. The estimate sits a hair above the line, and the honest interval runs from well below 20% to well above it. Read plainly, the survey cannot settle the call. That is not a failure of the survey; it is a mismatch between the precision bought and the precision the decision needed.

LiveTolerable error is a property of the decision3 min▶

Ask the council one question before anyone looks at a margin: how far off could the number be before you would choose differently? If the bus would be kept at 20% and also at 15%, because the library extension is unaffordable either way, then a ±5.5 survey is more than enough and the vote is not really about the survey. If the call genuinely flips at 20%, the tolerable error is the distance between 20% and the values the council considers plausible, and that is usually only a few points.

Then read the result against it. The lab's R3 design, stratified by area and raked on age, sex and area, prints Estimate: 20.2% ± 5.5 (honest margin) · naive ±5.2 on sample 1. The interval covers values on both sides of 20%. Honest reporting says the survey cannot tell whether Ottervale is above or below the line. On the bench you can see the truth, 21.7%; the council cannot, which is the whole point.

Notice what the honest margin includes: the design effect from stratifying and weighting. The naive ±5.2 is narrower and wrong for this design. Leader session 4 covers putting the honest one in the report.

LiveThe price of a tighter answer2 min▶

The planner below starts at the settings Ottervale would use: an expected share of 22%, a 22% response rate and a design effect of 1.2. For ±3 points it asks for 733 completes at simple random, 879 after the design effect, and 3,996 addresses issued. For ±5 points: 264 completes, 317 after the design effect, 1,441 issued. Shrinking the margin by two points nearly triples the fieldwork.

Two things the planner cannot do. It plans precision only, so no size buys back a frame that misses people or answering that tracks the answer (Part 3). And it cannot promise the estimate will land away from the line: a result at 20.2% with ±3 would still straddle 20%. Agree in advance what the council does when the survey lands inside the margin of the threshold, for example treat it as a tie and decide on other grounds. Writing that rule before fieldwork is cheaper than arguing it after.

Real world

Precision is a disclosure item, not a courtesy. The AAPOR Transparency Initiative asks every released survey to state its completes and its precision: a margin of error for a probability sample, a precision statement for a non-probability one. A council can turn that around: write the precision the decision needs into the brief, and treat a delivered margin wider than that as a survey that cannot carry the call. (AAPOR, Transparency Initiative disclosure elements, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers subgroup cells too small to report

Cells too thin to print

5 min live

The town-wide number is built from about two hundred answers. The moment a councillor asks "and what about my area?", the number is built from far fewer. On the lab's R3 sample 1, the widget prints Answers per area: fewest 9 (area 1) · most 42 (area 8). A per-area share from 9 answers carries a margin far too wide to act on, and each of those 9 people is more than a tenth of their area's figure. Decide the smallest cell you will report before fieldwork, and merge or suppress below it.

LiveSet the minimum before anyone sees a number3 min▶

A minimum cell size is a rule that says: below this many answers, we do not report the group's figure on its own. There is no universal number, and this page does not give you one. The council has to choose its own, and the right moment is before fieldwork, for two reasons. First, once a figure exists, someone will want it, and refusing a number that has been seen looks like hiding it. Second, a minimum chosen after the results are in can be chosen to keep the convenient cells and drop the awkward ones.

Choose it from the decision, as in Part 1: what is the widest margin at which a per-area figure would still change what the council does in that area? Then, below the minimum, either merge (report area 1 together with a neighbour) or suppress (print "too few answers to report"). Both are honest. Printing area 1's share from 9 people as if it were comparable to the town-wide figure is not.

If per-area figures matter to the decision, that belongs in the brief from Leader session 2: the planner in Part 1 sizes the whole sample, and a design that needs reliable area figures needs enough completes in the smallest area, not just in total.

Self-studyWhy the small cell pulls so hard3 min▶

Weighting makes each person stand for a number of residents. In a big cell, a single unusual answer is diluted among dozens of others. In a cell of 9, it is not: one person changes the area's figure by more than a tenth of its width, and if their weight is also large, by more again. That is the weight that pulls too hard. It is the same mechanism Analyst session 5 shows for the whole sample, concentrated in one area.

Small cells raise a second problem in people data: a cut small enough can identify the person behind the answer. Employee surveys use a minimum group size for anonymity as well as precision, taught in People Analytics, a5 ↗. A resident survey cut by area and age band can hit the same wall.

Real world

Capping a weight is a trade, not a fix. Kalton and Flores-Cervantes describe bounding or trimming large weights as a way to improve precision, at some cost in the bias reduction the weights were there to deliver. The same trade sits under every small cell: you can stop one person from pulling too hard, but only by letting the cell represent its residents less exactly. (Kalton and Flores-Cervantes, Journal of Official Statistics 19(2), 2003, read at source.)

Part 3 · covers when a survey is the wrong tool

When to put the survey down

5 min live

A survey answers one kind of question well: roughly how many people in a defined population hold a view or are in a situation, when the people asked are reasonably likely to answer regardless of their answer. Change any part of that and the survey loses its footing. Three cases come up often enough to name: the people the decision is about are the least likely to answer; records already hold the answer; and the question is why, not how many.

LiveWhen the people it is about are the ones who do not answer2 min▶

The bus route matters most to people who would be cut off without it. If those same people are harder to reach and less inclined to fill in a council form, the survey under-counts exactly the group the decision turns on. The lab's R5 rung simulates this: answering is tied to the answer itself, so people in worse health answer less within every age group. It answers at about the same rate as R3, 227 against 226 of 1,000 issued, yet prints Estimate: 9.4% ± 3.6 (honest margin) · naive ±3.8 against a truth of 21.7%.

Over 1,000 repeats R5's bias is -8.9 points and its honest margin covers the truth 5% of the time. Raking on age, sex and area does not help, because the missing people are missing inside those groups. The response rate gives no warning: it looks just like R3's. When there is a plausible reason the people the decision is about would be the least likely to answer, a survey is the wrong first tool. Go to them: a targeted study through the services they use, or records that count them without asking.

LiveWhen records already hold it, or the question is why2 min▶

Records. Suppose Ottervale's bus operator already logs pass use and ticket sales by stop (a constructed example; no figures are given). Those records count the people who actually ride, including the ones who would never answer a form, with no sampling error and no nonresponse. They have their own gaps, such as people who would ride if the timetable suited them, but if the council's question is "who uses this route", asking a sample is the slower, noisier way to learn something already written down.

Why, not how many. A survey describes. It can tell the council roughly what share of adults would be cut off; it cannot tell them why people stopped using the library, or whether longer hours would bring them back. Those are questions about causes, and they need interviews to find the reasons and a causal design to test them. Leader session 4 shows how to report a survey so it is read as description, and Learn causal inference with Phoebe ↗ covers the designs that can carry a "why".

Self-studyThe response-rate argument, left open3 min▶

It is tempting to read R5 as "low response rates ruin surveys". The evidence does not settle that, and serious researchers disagree.

One side: Pew's 2012 comparison ran a standard 5-day survey (1,507 interviews, 9% response) alongside a high-effort survey fielded from 5 January to 15 March (2,226 interviews, 22% response, with incentives and refusal-conversion letters). Of 40 comparisons, 28 differed by 2 points or less. Groves and Peytcheva's meta-analysis of 59 studies is reported to have found the response rate a weak predictor of bias.

The other side: Groves (2006) argues that nonresponse can, but need not, cause bias, and that what matters is how strongly answering is linked to the thing measured. On that view the rate is the wrong gauge, and the same Pew comparison found 7 measures differing by 3 points and 5 by 4. The lab shows the mechanism without resolving the argument: at the same rate, evenly spread nonresponse is harmless (bias -0.3) and answer-linked nonresponse is not (bias -8.9).

For a leader the practical point survives either way: ask whether there is a reason answering would track the answer, not just what the rate was.

Real world

Most measures held, some moved. In Pew's 2012 test, doubling down on effort (ten weeks of fieldwork, incentives, letters to people who had refused) lifted the response rate from 9% to 22%. Across 40 comparisons, 28 moved by 2 points or less, 7 by 3 and 5 by 4. Whether that reassures you depends on whether your decision lives in the 28 or the 5, which is exactly the question Part 1 asks. (Pew Research Center, "Assessing the representativeness of public opinion surveys", 2012, read at source.)

★ Step 5 · Ottervale decision memo, v1 (constructed)1 Decision: keep the bus route if at least 20% of adults would be cut off without it (constructed rule) 2 Error budget: the call flips at 20%; we can tolerate +/- ___ points if inside the margin of 20%, we will: ____________ 3 What it carries: town-wide estimate 20.2% +/- 5.5 (honest margin); it does / does not settle the call: ______ 4 Cells not to report: areas below ___ answers (set before fieldwork); area 1 has 9: merge with ______ or suppress 5 Do not use the survey if: ______________________________ (the people cut off are the least likely to answer; records such as pass and ticket data hold it; the question is why, not how many)

A memo that says "the survey cannot settle this" is a good memo. It stops the council quoting 20.2% as if it meant "above the line", and it moves the argument to where it belongs: the other evidence, the cost of each option, and a tie rule agreed in advance.

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The council keeps the bus if at least 20% of adults would be cut off without it. The survey prints 20.2% ± 5.5. What should the memo say?

An interval that straddles the decision line says the call is close, not which way it goes. The naive margin ignores the design effect and is wrong for a weighted design.

2 · On the lab's R3 sample 1, area 1 has 9 answers and area 8 has 42. A councillor asks for area 1's figure. What is the honest response?

A figure from 9 answers carries a margin far too wide to act on, and each person is more than a tenth of it. The minimum belongs before fieldwork, so it cannot be chosen to keep convenient cells.

3 · R5 answers at 23%, the same as R3, yet its honest margin covers the truth only 5% of the time. What does that teach about when a survey is the wrong tool?

The damage comes from answering being linked to the answer, not from the rate. Size narrows the margin around the wrong value; weights cannot see who is missing inside a group.
