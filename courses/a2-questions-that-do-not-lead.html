Questions that do not lead: the answer starts in the wording

A perfect sample still returns the wrong number if the question pushes people toward an answer, asks two things at once, or offers a scale that leans one way. This is measurement error, and it is the one error a bigger sample, a better list or a cleverer weight cannot touch. Tonight you learn to spot the faults that cause it, with published experiments that show how far wording and order move an answer, and you rewrite the cabinet member's draft questionnaire for Ottervale, a constructed town council, into a version 2 you can pretest.

Part 0

Where we start

In Analyst session 1 the error budget carried a row called measurement, marked "not measured". This session fills it. The survey error lab sets measurement error to zero on purpose: it asks every one of its 20,000 census residents the census health question exactly as the census asked it, so nothing in the wording can move the answer. That is why there is no lab on this page. Real questionnaires are written by people with a view, under deadline, and the faults they carry are predictable enough to find with a checklist and a small pretest.

Part 1 · covers wording

Five faults that put the answer in the question

5 min live

A survey question is an instrument, and a bent instrument reads high or low every time. The common bends are few: a cue that tells the respondent which answer is wanted, a premise that loads the dice, two questions sharing one answer box, a frequency word every reader defines differently, and a term the respondent has to guess at. Under all of them sits a quieter error: asking about something other than the concept the decision needs.

LiveFive faults to look for first4 min▶

Leading. The item signals the expected answer: "don't you agree", "most residents feel", a description like "vital" attached to one option. Fix: state the topic and offer both directions in the stem, "support or oppose", "important, if at all".

Loaded. A premise does the persuading before the question starts: "given rising costs, would you accept cutting the bus?" Every respondent is handed the council's argument and asked to agree with it. Fix: drop the premise, or give both sides' reasons in matched form.

Double-barrelled. Two objects or two judgements share one answer, joined by "and" or "or". A "yes" could mean yes to both or yes to the one the respondent cared about. Fix: one item per object, as in the figure above.

Vague quantifier. "Regularly", "often", "a lot" mean daily to one resident and monthly to another, so the same behaviour lands in different boxes. Fix: a reference period and a count, "in the past four weeks, on how many days".

Undefined term. "Council services", "public transport", "the library offer": if the respondent has to guess what is included, different respondents answer different questions. Fix: name what counts, or ask about a specific thing.

Self-studySpecification: asking about the decision's concept3 min▶

A question can be perfectly neutral and still measure the wrong thing. Ottervale's council must choose between keeping the route 7 bus and extending library hours. "How important is the bus to you?" and "How important are library hours to you?" will both come back mostly "important", because almost everything is important when it costs the respondent nothing to say so. The decision is a trade-off, so the questionnaire needs at least one item that asks for the trade: if the council can fund only one, which should it be.

Work backwards from the decision memo. Write the sentence the council wants to be able to say ("most residents who depend on the bus would rather keep it than have longer library hours") and check that some item, or some pair of items, can support it. If none can, the error is specification, and no amount of neutral wording fixes it. Analyst session 1 named this error; here it becomes a test every draft item has to pass.

Real world

One clause, a different majority. In January 2003 Pew Research Center asked Americans whether they would favour military action in Iraq. Asked plainly, 68% favoured it and 25% opposed. When the item added the clause "even if it meant that U.S. forces might suffer thousands of casualties" (Pew Research Center), 43% favoured and 48% opposed. Both versions were honest questions; they measured different concepts, and the clause decided which one. (Pew Research Center, "Writing survey questions", read at source.)

Part 2 · covers response options

The answer options are part of the question

5 min live

Respondents read the answer list as a hint about what is normal. A scale with three good options and one bad one tells them the expected answer is good; a list with no middle and no "don't know" makes an undecided resident pick a side the designer chose for them. The options decide where uncertainty goes, so they deserve the same scrutiny as the stem.

LiveBalanced scales, the midpoint and don't know4 min▶

Balance first. A rating scale should have as many steps on the negative side as on the positive, with labels that mirror each other: very good and very poor, fairly good and fairly poor. This is the uncontested part. An unbalanced scale shifts answers toward the heavy side, and the report then reads the shift as opinion.

The midpoint and don't know are a genuine design trade. One camp of designers includes a middle option and a separate "don't know" so that people without an opinion are not forced to invent one; a forced answer from an undecided resident is noise dressed up as a view. Another camp omits them, or tucks them away, because an easy exit also attracts people who do have a view but do not want to work it out, which is the low-effort answering described below. Both positions are held by careful practitioners. Neither is right for every item, and no single effect size settles it, so the professional move is to decide per item, say what you decided in the methods note, and check it in the pretest.

Keep don't know separate. If you offer it, put it apart from the scale, as in the figure, so it does not read as a point between "fairly poor" and "very poor", and report it as its own share rather than dropping it silently.

Self-studyAgree and disagree items, acquiescence and satisficing4 min▶

Agree-disagree items ("The library's hours meet my needs: strongly agree to strongly disagree") are cheap to write and fast to answer, which is the problem. Some respondents tend to agree with whatever statement is put to them, a pattern called acquiescence, so the share agreeing depends partly on which way the statement was written. The safer form names the dimension and offers both ends: "How well, if at all, do the library's hours meet your needs?"

Jon Krosnick's 1991 paper describes a broader behaviour he called satisficing: when answering well takes more effort than a respondent wants to give, they settle for an answer that is merely acceptable, such as picking the first reasonable option, agreeing, or choosing whatever keeps things as they are (Krosnick, Applied Cognitive Psychology, 1991, reported from secondary sources, not read in full). Long grids, late items in a long questionnaire and agree-disagree batteries all make satisficing easier. Shorter questionnaires, item-specific options and varied formats make it harder.

Real world

A name for the low-effort answer. Krosnick's satisficing account (reported) gives questionnaire writers a simple test for every item: what is the easiest answer here, and would a tired respondent give it regardless of their view? If the easiest answer is "agree", "the first option" or "no change", the item is measuring effort as well as opinion. Ottervale's draft puts its most important item, the trade-off between the bus and the library, last, where effort is lowest.

Part 3 · covers order and context, and pretesting

The question before sets up the question after

5 min live

Respondents answer each item in the light of the ones before it. A preceding question brings certain facts to mind, and the next answer is built from what is already in mind. So an identical item can return a different number depending only on its position. The defence has two parts: order the questionnaire deliberately, and test the order before fielding it.

LiveOrdering a questionnaire on purpose3 min▶

General before specific. An overall rating ("how satisfied are you with council services") asked after a specific item about bus cuts is answered with the bus cuts in mind. Asked first, it gets the respondent's own overall view. Ottervale's draft has exactly this problem.

Neutral before contentious. Behaviour and experience items (how often you use the bus, when you last visited the library) are easier and less loaded than opinion items, and they put relevant facts in mind evenly for every respondent.

Watch adjacent pairs. When two items share a topic, as Pew's marriage and legal-agreement items did, the first can make the second look like a compromise or a contrast. Separate them, or test both orders.

Rotate when there is no right order. If several items have equal claim to go first, rotating their order across respondents spreads the context effect instead of fixing it in one direction, and lets you measure it.

Self-studyCognitive pretesting: the cheapest defence4 min▶

Every fault on this page is easier to catch with five conversations than with five thousand completes. A cognitive pretest puts the draft in front of a handful of people like the respondents and watches how they answer it.

Think-aloud. The respondent says what they are thinking while they answer. You hear where they hesitate, what they think a term means and which answer they nearly gave.

Probing. After an item, you ask targeted follow-ups: what did "regularly" mean to you, which journeys were you counting, was the answer you wanted on the list. Write the probes before the session so every respondent gets the same ones.

Split-ballot test. On a small sample, half get one wording or order and half get the other. A small split will not give you a precise effect, but it will tell you whether the two versions behave very differently, which is the thing you need to know before fielding.

This page covers the pretest only. Running interviews as research in their own right, recruiting for them and coding what people say, is taught in AI research, qualitative coding ↗.

Real world

A compromise that depends on what came first. In October 2003 Pew Research Center asked about legal agreements for same-sex couples that would give many of the same rights as married couples. Asked straight after a question on same-sex marriage, 45% favoured them; asked without that question first, 37% did. Next to the marriage item, legal agreements read as a middle way; on their own, they did not. (Pew Research Center, "Writing survey questions", read at source.)

Build-along

Rewrite the cabinet member's draft into questionnaire v2

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The cabinet member for transport has sent over a six-item draft for the resident survey that will inform the bus route versus library hours decision. It is a constructed Ottervale example, written to carry one of each fault from tonight. Work the five steps in a text editor or on paper. The goal is not elegant prose: it is items that measure what the council needs to decide, in an order that does not push the answer.

Step 1 · Paste the draft. Copy the cabinet member's six items into your working file exactly as written, options included. Do not fix anything yet; the tagging in step 2 is easier on the raw text.

★ Step 1 · Ottervale resident survey, draft 1 (constructed)Q1 Given rising costs, would you accept cutting the route 7 bus? Yes / No Q2 How satisfied are you with council services overall? Very satisfied / Fairly satisfied / Neither / Fairly dissatisfied / Very dissatisfied Q3 Don't you agree the council should protect the vital route 7 bus and the library? Yes / No Q4 How would you rate the library's opening hours? Excellent / Very good / Good / Poor Q5 Do you use the bus regularly? Yes / No Q6 Which should the council fund: the route 7 bus or longer library hours? The bus / The library

Step 2 · Tag each item with its fault. One main fault per item, in the vocabulary from Parts 1 to 3. Some items carry a second fault; note it, but fix the main one first.

★ Step 2 · the fault tagsQ1 leading and loaded: the premise "rising costs" plus "accept" frames the cut as reasonable Q2 order: sits straight after a bus-cuts item, so "council services" is rated with cuts in mind Q3 double-barrelled: two objects, one answer (also leading: "Don't you agree", "vital") Q4 unbalanced scale: three positive options, one negative, no middle Q5 vague frequency: "regularly" has no reference period and no count Q6 no don't know: undecided residents, and those who want both or neither, must pick a side

Step 3 · Rewrite items 1 to 3. Neutral stems, both directions offered, one object per item. Q2's wording is sound; its problem is position, which step 4 fixes.

★ Step 3 · items 1 to 3, version 2Q1 Would you support or oppose ending the route 7 bus service? Strongly support / Somewhat support / Neither / Somewhat oppose / Strongly oppose / Don't know Q2 How satisfied are you with the services Ottervale council provides overall? Very satisfied / Fairly satisfied / Neither / Fairly dissatisfied / Very dissatisfied / Don't know Q3a How important, if at all, is keeping the route 7 bus to you? Very / Fairly / Not very / Not at all / Don't know Q3b How important, if at all, is extending library opening hours to you? Very / Fairly / Not very / Not at all / Don't know

Step 4 · Rewrite items 4 to 6, then reorder. Balance the scale, give the frequency item a period and a count, give the trade-off item honest exits. Then put the whole questionnaire in order: general before specific, behaviour before opinion, the trade-off last but not buried behind a long grid.

★ Step 4 · items 4 to 6, and the v2 orderQ4 How would you rate the library's current opening hours? Very good / Fairly good / Neither / Fairly poor / Very poor [separate] Don't know Q5 In the past four weeks, on how many days did you travel on the route 7 bus? None / 1 to 3 days / 4 to 8 days / 9 or more days / Can't remember Q6 If the council can fund only one of these next year, which should it be? Keep the route 7 bus / Extend library hours / No preference / Don't know Questionnaire v2 order: 1 Q2 overall satisfaction (general, asked before any specific service) 2 Q5 bus use, past four weeks (behaviour) 3 Q4 library hours rating 4 Q3a importance of the bus 5 Q3b importance of library hours (rotate 4 and 5 across respondents) 6 Q1 support or oppose ending route 7 7 Q6 the trade-off the decision needs

Step 5 · Write a three-probe pretest script for two items. Pick the two items most likely to be misread. For Ottervale that is Q5, where "the route 7 bus" and "days" can be counted many ways, and Q6, the item the decision rests on. Run the script with a handful of residents before fielding; your findings will differ from anyone else's, so record what each person actually said.

★ Step 5 · cognitive pretest script (constructed)Opening: "Please read each question aloud and tell me what you are thinking as you answer." Q5 bus days, past four weeks P1 comprehension: Which journeys did you count as the route 7 bus? P2 retrieval: How did you work out the number of days? P3 judgement: How sure are you of that number? Was there a box you nearly picked? Q6 the trade-off P1 comprehension: In your own words, what is this question asking you to choose? P2 judgement: What were you weighing up when you chose? P3 options: Was the answer you wanted on the list? If not, what was it? Record per item: what was misread __________ what to change in v2 __________

Pretest the order, not only the items. If you are unsure whether Q2 still picks up the bus topic from the invitation letter or the survey title, a small split-ballot pretest with Q2 first for half and later for the other half will show whether the two versions behave very differently. It will not give a precise effect; it will tell you whether to worry.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Pew's January 2003 item on military action in Iraq drew 68% in favour asked plainly and 43% with a clause about thousands of casualties. What does that show?

Both versions were honest questions about different things. The number belongs to the wording, which is why the exact wording is a disclosure item and why specification comes first.

2 · Ottervale's draft rates the library's hours as Excellent / Very good / Good / Poor. What is the main problem?

A balanced scale mirrors its labels on both sides. A larger sample measures the lean more precisely; it does not remove it.

3 · In Pew's December 2008 survey, 88% said they were dissatisfied with the way things were going when the item came after a presidential approval question, and 78% without it. What is the practical lesson?

The first question brought its topic to mind. In Ottervale's draft, the overall council-services item sat right after a bus-cuts item; v2 moves it to the front.
