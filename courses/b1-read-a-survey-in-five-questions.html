Learn Survey Design & Sampling with Phoebe · Leader session 1 of 4

Read a survey in five questions: before you act on the number

A survey lands on your desk with a headline and a percentage. Whether it can carry the decision in front of you depends on five things the headline never says: who the survey meant to describe, who it could reach, how it picked them, who answered and how the answers were weighted. The answers usually sit in a methods box at the back, in small type. Tonight you learn to read that box first, on a draft report for Ottervale, a constructed town council deciding whether to keep a bus route.

Part 1 · covers the five questions

The headline is the last thing to read

5 min live

Every survey number is the end of a chain: a population, a list that reaches part of it, a way of picking from that list, the people who answered, and the weights that push those answers back toward the population. Each link can tilt the number, and the margin of error printed next to it describes only the picking. Five questions, one per link, tell you how far the number can be trusted.

LiveThe five questions, and a worrying answer to each4 min▶

1 · Who is the population? The group the number claims to describe. "Residents" is vague; "adults aged 16 and over living in Ottervale" is a population. Worrying: the report never says, or the population quietly changes between the headline and the methods box.

2 · Who could be reached? The list or method that defines who had any chance of being asked, called the frame. Worrying: an online panel described as "residents", or a household address list used to describe people in care homes.

3 · How were they picked? At random from the list, or by whoever volunteered or walked past. Worrying: a margin of error printed for a sample nobody picked at random.

4 · Who answered? How many were asked, how many answered, and whether the answerers differ from the rest. Worrying: no response rate, or a rate with no word on who is missing.

5 · How was it weighted? Which variables the answers were adjusted to, and to what totals. Worrying: "weighted to be representative" with no variables named.

Self-studyReading numbers in general lives elsewhere2 min▶

How to read a chart, a percentage change or a base rate is general data literacy, taught in Data Literacy ↗. Engagement and pulse surveys inside an organisation, with their small-team anonymity rules, are taught in People Analytics, surveys as evidence ↗. The five questions here are the survey-specific layer on top: what a sample of people can say about everyone else.

Real world

The profession publishes its own reading list. The American Association for Public Opinion Research runs a Transparency Initiative whose member organisations disclose, for every survey, items including the sponsor and who ran it, the exact wording, the population, how the sample was generated (frame, probability or not, quotas, incentives), mode, dates, completed interviews with a precision statement, and the weighting or a statement that none was used. Four of the five questions map straight onto that list. (AAPOR, Transparency Initiative, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers who answered

A low response rate is a question, not a verdict

5 min live

Question four is where most readers stop thinking: a low response rate sounds disqualifying, a high one sounds safe. Neither follows. What matters is whether the people who answered differ from those who did not on the thing being measured. A survey can lose four in five people and stay close to the truth, or lose a few and be badly off.

LiveTwo camps, both with evidence4 min▶

One camp reads the Pew comparison, and a 2008 meta-analysis by Groves and Peytcheva of 59 studies that measured nonresponse bias directly, as evidence that the response rate is a weak guide to bias (the weak relationship is reported from a summary of that meta-analysis). The other camp points out that weak on average is not zero for your survey: Pew's own comparison found differences of 4 points on five measures, and a survey about civic life, volunteering or health can lose exactly the people it is about. Both camps agree on the move a reader should make: ask what the survey did to check who was missing, not just how many.

Self-studyResponse rates have rules2 min▶

"Response rate" is not one number. AAPOR's Standard Definitions, now in their tenth edition (2023), define a family of response, cooperation, refusal and contact rates with different treatment of people whose eligibility was never established. When a report quotes a rate, ask which one; two honest surveys can report different rates for the same fieldwork.

Real world

The rate fell, the polls kept working, mostly. Pew reports that its typical phone survey's response rate fell from 36% in 1997 to 9% in 2012, and its high-effort comparison found most estimates stable. The same report is the source the other camp quotes for the measures that did move. (Pew Research Center, "Assessing the representativeness of public opinion surveys", 2012, read at source.)

Part 3 · covers the margin and the sample size

A bigger sample is a tighter guess, not a better one

5 min live

The most persuasive line in a survey report is often the sample size. Four thousand respondents sounds more trustworthy than a thousand, and the margin of error agrees. But size buys down only the error from picking at random. If the people who answer lean one way, a bigger sample leans the same way, more confidently. The survey error lab shows it on real census rows.

LiveWhat to ask when a report leads with its sample size3 min▶

Ask question four again. A big sample from a frame that misses people, or with answers that lean one way, is a precise measurement of the wrong group. The lab's 4,000-address survey prints ±2.8 and covers the truth on 3 of every 100 repeats. If the report cannot say who answered and how they were weighted, the sample size tells you how confident it is, not how right.

Self-studyWhen the margin is honest and when it is not2 min▶

A margin from a weighted, stratified or clustered survey should include the design effect, the extra variance the design and the weights add. Most press releases print the simple-random margin regardless. Analyst session 4 computes the honest one; Leader session 4 asks for it in the report.

Ask for the unweighted counts. A report that shows how many people answered in each age band and area lets you see question 4 for yourself. If 9 people answered in one area, no weight makes that area's number solid.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A report says "1,200 respondents, margin of error ± 3 points". Which of the five questions has it answered?

A margin of error is a statement about random selection. It says nothing about the population, the frame, who answered or the weights.

2 · Pew's 2012 high-effort survey reached a 22% response rate against 9% for the standard one. What did comparing them show?

28 of 40 comparisons were within 2 points. A low rate is a question to ask about who is missing, not a verdict either way.

3 · On the lab, the same unweighted design with 4,000 addresses instead of 1,000 prints ± 2.8. Why should that not reassure you?

Size reduces only the error from random picking. If the people who answer lean one way, more of them lean the same way.
