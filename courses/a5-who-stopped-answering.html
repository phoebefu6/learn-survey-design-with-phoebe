Who stopped answering: and weighting them back

Fewer and fewer people answer surveys, and every report that prints a low response rate invites the same question: can the number still be trusted? Survey methodologists have argued about it for two decades, with good evidence on both sides. Tonight you take that argument apart on the bench, where the same response rate can hide no bias at all or nine points of it, and then you do the repair work: post-stratify, rake, trim, and pay for each weight in a wider margin. By the end Ottervale, a constructed town council, has a weighting spec it can defend.

Part 1 · covers response rates against nonresponse bias

Same response rate, three different biases

5 min live

A response rate tells you how many of the people you picked answered. Nonresponse bias is about who: whether the people who answered differ, on the thing you are measuring, from the people who did not. The two are related, but how strongly is one of the live arguments in survey methodology. Below, the lab holds the rate at roughly one in four and changes only who answers.

LiveSide A: low rates have not meant large bias2 min▶

Pew Research Center's 2012 study is the one most people cite. A typical Pew phone survey's response rate fell from 36% in 1997 to 9% in 2012. To test what that cost, Pew ran its standard five-day survey (1,507 interviews, 9% response) beside a high-effort survey fielded from 5 January to 15 March with incentives and refusal-conversion letters (2,226 interviews, 22% response). Of 40 comparisons, 28 differed by 2 points or less, 7 by 3 points and 5 by 4 points (read at source).

Two earlier lines of work point the same way. Keeter and colleagues (2006) repeated a 1997 experiment comparing a standard and a rigorous survey and found little to suggest nonresponse seriously threatened the estimates at the rates they obtained (reported). Groves and Peytcheva (2008) meta-analysed 59 methodological studies that measured nonresponse bias directly, using frame variables, record matches and follow-ups of nonrespondents (study count read at source in the abstract); the same analysis covered 959 estimates and found the response rate a weak predictor of bias (reported from a secondary summary).

LiveSide B: the rate was never the right measure2 min▶

Robert Groves' 2006 article makes the other half of the argument: nonresponse can, but need not, induce bias (abstract read at source). What decides it, in the propensity framing reported from summaries of the paper, is how strongly the chance of answering is linked to the survey variable itself. A low rate does not create bias on its own; it leaves more room for that link to matter. And Pew's own table is not all reassurance: 5 of its 40 measures moved by 4 points, which on a close council decision is the whole margin.

The bench row above is that mechanism made visible. At about the same one-in-four rate, answering spread evenly gives bias -0.3 and covers the truth 94% of the time; answering tied to age gives +5.2 and 58%; answering tied to the answer itself gives -8.9 and 5%. The lab cannot tell you which world a real survey is in. Neither can its response rate. That is why the debate is still open, and why a careful analyst reports both sides and the evidence each rests on.

Self-studyWhich response rate are you quoting?2 min▶

"Response rate" is not one number. AAPOR's Standard Definitions, now in its 10th edition (2023), define a family of response, cooperation, refusal and contact rates, which differ in how they treat partial interviews and cases whose eligibility was never settled. Two surveys can quote different rates from the same fieldwork. When you report one, name which AAPOR rate it is, and when you compare two surveys, check they used the same one. The formulas are in the Standard Definitions; read them there rather than from a summary.

Real world

From 36% to 9%, and mostly the same answers. Pew's 2012 comparison is the strongest evidence that low response rates need not wreck a survey, and its 4-point measures are the strongest reason not to stop checking. Both readings are honest. (Pew Research Center, "Assessing the representativeness of public opinion surveys", 2012, read at source; Groves, Public Opinion Quarterly 70(5), 2006, abstract read at source.)

Part 2 · covers post-stratification and raking

Weights put the missing residents back, one cell at a time

5 min live

If the young answer less, each young respondent has to speak for more young residents than an older respondent does. A weight is that ratio made explicit: how many people in the town this respondent stands for. Post-stratification sets one weight per cell of a cross-table, so the weighted sample matches the census in every cell. Raking matches only the margins, one variable at a time, and repeats until they all agree.

LivePost-stratification: twelve cells, twelve weights2 min▶

Cross age by sex and you get 12 cells. For each cell, the census tells you its share of Ottervale's adults and the respondents tell you its share of the answers. The weight for everyone in that cell is the first share divided by the second. The youngest cells, who answer least, get the largest weights; the oldest, who answer most, the smallest. After weighting, the sample matches the census exactly on all 12 cells.

On the bench, the post preset (stratified by area, post-stratified on age by sex) brings R2's bias of +5.3 down to -0.4, and the honest margin covers the truth 94% of the time over 1,000 repeats. The price shows on the design line: sample 1's design effect rises to 1.08.

LiveRaking: when the cross-table runs out of people2 min▶

Add area to age by sex and the cross-table has far more cells than a few hundred respondents can fill; some will be empty, and an empty cell cannot be weighted. Raking avoids the problem by matching only the margins. Adjust the weights so the age distribution matches the census; then adjust so sex matches, which knocks age slightly out; then area; then age again. Kalton and Flores-Cervantes (2003, read at source) describe this as iterative proportional fitting: row then column until the weights settle, usually fast, though convergence is not guaranteed.

R3 rakes Ottervale's respondents to age, sex and area. Over 1,000 repeats its bias is -0.4 and the honest margin covers 94%; its average split shows nonresponse +5.5 and weighting -5.6, the weights cancelling almost exactly what the age skew put in.

Self-studyWhy raking fixed R1, and when it will not2 min▶

Raking worked on R1's problem because the problem lived inside the weighting variables: older residents answered more, and older residents report poorer health more often. Once the weights restore the age mix, the health mix comes back with it. The condition is that, within each age, sex and area group, the people who answered resemble the people who did not on the thing being measured. When that fails, as in R5 where poorer health itself makes people less likely to answer, the weights match every census margin and the estimate is still far off. Weighting can only see what it is weighted on.

Real world

The method official statisticians reach for. Kalton and Flores-Cervantes' review of weighting methods sets out cell weighting, raking and their relatives side by side, and why raking is the usual choice when the sample is too small to fill a full cross-table but the population margins are known from a census or register. (Kalton and Flores-Cervantes, "Weighting methods", Journal of Official Statistics 19(2), 2003, read at source.)

Part 3 · covers trimming and the variance cost of weights

Every weight pulls the estimate in and pushes the margin out

5 min live

Weighting is not free. When some respondents count for three residents and others for half of one, the estimate leans on a few people, and it moves more from sample to sample. That shows up as a design effect above 1, a smaller effective sample, and an honest margin wider than the naive one. Trimming caps the largest weights to win some precision back, and pays for it with a little bias.

LiveRead the design line after every weighting step2 min▶

On sample 1, R3's 226 respondents carry a design effect of 1.10, so they are worth an effective sample of 206. Its honest margin is ±5.5 against a naive ±5.2 that pretends every respondent counted equally. The naive margin is the one most survey software prints by default, and it is too narrow whenever the weights vary.

A useful rule of thumb, from Kish (1992) as reported by Little and Vartivarian (2005): weighting raises the variance by roughly the squared coefficient of variation of the weights, so the design effect from weighting is about 1 plus that square. They note it holds best when the weighting variables are only weakly linked to the outcome (reported). The lab does not use the approximation; it computes the variance of each estimate directly from the weights. The interval mathematics itself is taught in Statistics, confidence intervals ↗.

LiveTrimming: cap the few, steady the many2 min▶

R4 rakes as R3 does, then caps every weight at twice the mean, rescales the rest so the total is kept, and repeats the pass; on sample 1 it trimmed 10 weights on its first pass. The design effect falls from 1.10 to 1.05, effective n rises from 206 to 214, and the honest margin narrows from ±5.5 to ±5.4. The cost is on the repeat line: bias moves from -0.4 to +0.5, because the capped respondents now speak for slightly fewer of the residents they were standing in for. Kalton and Flores-Cervantes (2003, read at source) describe exactly this trade: bounding or trimming weights improves precision at some cost in bias reduction.

Self-studyChoosing a cap you can defend2 min▶

Twice the mean is the lab's rule, not a law. Practitioners use caps at a multiple of the mean or median, or at a percentile of the weights, and the right one depends on how small the hardest-hit cell is and how much bias the decision can tolerate. Whatever you choose, write it down before fieldwork, report how many weights it touched, and show the design effect with and without it. A cap chosen after seeing which version gives the preferred answer is not a weighting rule; it is a thumb on the scale.

Real world

Does weighting for nonresponse always cost variance? Little and Vartivarian (2005) asked exactly that, and used Kish's cv-squared approximation as their starting point; their answer is that the cost depends on how the weighting variables relate to the outcome, which is the same condition that decides whether weighting removes bias at all. (Little and Vartivarian, Survey Methodology 31(2), 2005; the Kish approximation is reported from their paper.)

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the lab, "22% answer, evenly" gives bias -0.3; answering linked to the answer gives -8.9 at about the same rate. What does that show?

This is Groves' 2006 point made visible. Pew 2012 shows low rates often do little harm; the bench shows why they sometimes do a lot. Both stand.

2 · Raking on age, sex and area takes R1's design from bias +5.2 to R3's -0.4. Why did it work here?

Weighting fixes what it is weighted on. R5, raked the same way, still misses by 8.9 points because the skew there is on the answer itself.

3 · R4 caps weights at twice the mean. Compared with R3, what does it trade?

Bounding weights improves precision at some cost in bias reduction, as Kalton and Flores-Cervantes describe; the lab prints both sides of the trade.
