Learn Survey Design & Sampling with Phoebe · Analyst session 4 of 6

Designs that spend the budget well: precision per pound

Once the list is chosen, the design decides how the budget turns into precision. A sorted list picked every k-th entry spreads the sample for free; strata narrow the margin only when the strata differ on the answer; clusters cut the fieldwork bill and quietly shrink what each answer is worth. Tonight you price each one on real census rows standing in for Ottervale, a constructed town council, and you meet the trap every budget falls into once: buying a bigger sample to fix a bias.

Part 1 · covers simple random and systematic samples

Simple random, and every k-th name on a sorted list

5 min live

A simple random sample gives every entry on the frame the same chance and every pair of entries the same chance of being picked together. It is the baseline every other design is measured against: its variance is the denominator of the design effect. A systematic sample takes a random start and then every k-th entry, where k is the frame size divided by the sample size. On a list sorted by area it spreads the picks across areas without you asking, which is why survey practitioners call it implicit stratification. On a list with a repeating pattern it can fail badly.

LiveSimple random, then every k-th3 min▶

Simple random. Number the frame, let a random draw choose the entries. On the bench this is R1: the address list, 1,000 issued, older residents answering more. It is the reference design. Its spread over 1,000 repeat surveys is 3.0 points, and every other design on this page is compared with that.

Systematic. With 19,549 entries and 1,000 to issue, k is 19,549 divided by 1,000. Pick a random start between 1 and k, then take every k-th address down the list, rounding the step as you go. One random number decides the whole sample, so it is quick to run by hand and easy to audit. If the list is sorted by area, then by street, the sample cannot bunch in one corner of town: every stretch of the list gets its share.

No bench numbers here. The lab offers simple random, stratified and clustered designs, not systematic. Treat a systematic sample from a well-sorted list as behaving at least as well as a stratified one on the sort variable, and check the list for a pattern before you trust it.

Why a sample's estimate wanders, and how that spread shrinks with size, is taught in Statistics, sampling distributions ↗. Here the spread is taken as given and the question is which design buys the least of it.

Self-studyWhen systematic goes wrong3 min▶

A systematic sample is only as random as the list's order. The failure is periodicity: the list repeats a pattern on a cycle that matches k, or divides into it. A housing register that runs through every block floor by floor, a staff list in teams of ten with the manager first, a delivery log with one row per weekday: in each, the random start chooses which kind of entry you get, and then you get only that kind.

The check takes five minutes. Look at how the list is sorted. Read the first 50 entries and look for a cycle. If there is one, either re-sort the list on something meaningful (area, then street, then house number), or break the cycle by drawing a fresh random start every few hundred entries. Write the sort order into the methods statement: a reader cannot judge a systematic sample without it.

Real world

The population on the bench is itself a published sample. The Office for National Statistics released a teaching file holding 1% of the person records from Census 2021 for England and Wales, with 19 variables and region as its finest geography. The bench draws its 20,000 Ottervale stand-ins from the adults in that file with a fixed seed, so anyone can rebuild the same town and check the same truth. A sample design is only reproducible when the draw is written down. (Office for National Statistics, Public microdata teaching sample, England and Wales: Census 2021, 2023, Open Government Licence v3.0, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers stratified samples

Strata pay only when they differ on the answer

5 min live

Stratifying splits the frame into groups before you draw, here Ottervale's ten areas, and samples each one separately, usually in proportion to its size. Chance can no longer give one area too many addresses and another too few. What that removes from the error is the variation between areas. If the areas differ a lot on the answer, that is a large slice and the margin narrows. If they barely differ, there is little to remove, and stratifying by area buys almost nothing.

LiveR1 against R2: what stratifying by area bought3 min▶

R2 is R1 with one change: the 1,000 addresses are issued area by area in proportion to each area's size. Over 1,000 repeat surveys, the spread falls from 3.0 points to 2.9. The bias does not move: about +5.2 for R1 and +5.3 for R2, because older residents still answer more and nothing in the design touches that.

Why so little? The bench's truth is the share of adults in fair, bad or very bad health, and that share barely differs between Ottervale's ten areas (the nine English regions and Wales, standing in). With little difference between strata, there is little between-area variation for stratifying to remove. On a question where areas genuinely differ, say how far each area is from the bus depot, the same design would narrow the margin much more.

On sample 1, R2's design effect prints as 0.92 and its effective n as 245 from 226 answers: better than a simple random sample of the same size. Read that as one draw where chance helped as well as the design. The repeat line, 2.9 against 3.0, is the design's real record.

Self-studyBoosting a small area: a trade, not a free lunch3 min▶

On R2's sample 1, area 1 returns only 9 answers. If the council needs a number for area 1 on its own, 9 answers cannot carry it. Disproportionate allocation fixes that: issue more addresses in area 1 than its share of the town, so it returns enough answers to report.

The price comes at the town level. Area 1's residents are now over-represented, so each one must be weighted down to put the town back in proportion, and unequal weights widen the margin of every town-wide estimate. You buy a usable area figure with a larger design effect overall. The lab has no boost option, so there is no bench number for the size of that cost; your numbers will differ with how hard you boost. Decide which estimate the decision rests on, the town or the area, and allocate for that one.

Real world

What unequal weights cost, by a rule of thumb. Leslie Kish's approximation, reported in a later paper by Roderick Little and Sonya Vartivarian, says weighting raises the variance by roughly the squared coefficient of variation of the weights, so the design effect from weighting is about one plus that. It works best when the weighting variable is only weakly tied to the answer. A boosted area makes the weights unequal on purpose, and this rule gives a first estimate of what the boost will cost the town-wide margin. The lab computes its design effect directly rather than by the rule. (Kish 1992, reported in Little and Vartivarian, Survey Methodology 31(2), 2005.)

Part 3 · covers cluster samples, design effect and effective sample size

Clusters save fieldwork and spend effective answers

5 min live

An interviewer who knocks on ten doors on one street does a day's work in an afternoon. That is the case for a cluster sample: draw streets first, then residents within each street. The catch is that neighbours resemble each other. Ten answers from one street repeat part of the same information, so they are worth fewer than ten answers from ten streets. The design effect puts a number on that loss, and the effective sample size tells you what your answers are really worth.

LiveDesign effect and effective n, as the lab computes them4 min▶

Design effect = the honest variance of your estimate divided by the variance a simple random sample with the same number of answers would have had. Above 1, the design cost you precision. Below 1, it bought you some.

Effective sample size = respondents divided by the design effect. It answers the question every budget holder should ask: how many simple random answers is this design really worth?

The cluster preset is R1 with the design switched to "Clustered by street": the address list, 1,000 issued, older residents answering more, no weighting. On sample 1, 223 answer, the design effect is 1.23 and the effective n is 181. The naive margin a calculator would print is ±6.0; the honest margin, computed from the actual spread between streets, is ±6.6. Over 1,000 repeats the honest margin covers the truth 63% of the time and the naive one 58%. Neither reaches 95%, because the bias is still +5.2: clustering changes the variance, and nonresponse is untouched.

Self-studyThe textbook approximation, and the big-sample trap4 min▶

The textbook approximation. For equal-sized clusters, the design effect from clustering is approximately 1 + (cluster size - 1) x intra-cluster correlation, where the intra-cluster correlation measures how much more alike two people in the same cluster are than two people drawn at random. It shows why a small correlation still matters: with 10 per street, even a modest correlation is multiplied by 9. Use it to plan before fieldwork, from a correlation you can defend. After fieldwork, compute the design effect from the data, as the lab does, and report that. Taking fewer residents per street and more streets lowers the cost in precision and raises the cost in travel; the right split depends on both prices.

The big-sample trap. Set the lab to simple random with 4,000 addresses issued, older residents still answering more, no weighting: the big preset. The margin narrows to ±2.8 and the spread over 1,000 repeats falls to 1.4 points. The bias stays at +5.2. A tight interval around the wrong centre covers the truth in 3% of repeats. Four times the budget bought a more confident wrong answer, because sample size buys down sampling error and nothing else. Analyst session 5 goes after the bias itself.

Real world

Three quarters of a million interviews, still unrepresentative. Wei Wang, David Rothschild, Sharad Goel and Andrew Gelman analysed opt-in polls run on the Xbox gaming platform in the 45 days before the 2012 US presidential election: 750,148 interviews from 345,858 people, far from representative on age and sex. Size alone did not fix that. Only after multilevel regression and poststratification did their estimates line up with the leading poll aggregators. Whether such modelling makes opt-in samples fit for population estimates is still argued: AAPOR's 2010 report on online panels advises against them for that goal, and its 2013 task force calls model-based methods the most promising; Leader session 2 lays out both sides. (Wang et al., International Journal of Forecasting 31(3), 2015, read at source.)

Price the design, then the size. The cluster design turned 223 answers into the precision of 181, which may be worth it if travel is the biggest line in the fieldwork quote. Choose the design for precision per pound, and spend the rest of the budget on the error the design does not touch.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the bench, stratifying by area moves the spread over 1,000 repeats from 3.0 to only 2.9 points. Why so little?

Stratifying removes the variation between strata from the error. It pays as much as the strata differ on the answer, and Ottervale's areas are nearly level on this question.

2 · The cluster preset prints "design effect 1.23 · effective n 181" from 223 answers. What does that mean?

Design effect is honest variance over simple random variance; effective n is respondents divided by it. Neighbours resemble each other, so clustered answers repeat part of the same information.

3 · With 4,000 addresses issued, the margin narrows to ±2.8 but the honest margin covers the truth in only 3% of 1,000 repeats. Why did the bigger sample not help?

Bias does not shrink with size. A tighter interval around the same wrong centre is more confident and more often wrong.
