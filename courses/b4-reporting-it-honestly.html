Part 1 · covers the full margin

Print the margin the design earned

5 min live

Most survey reports print a margin of error worked out as if the sample were a simple random draw. Ottervale's plan is not one: it is stratified by area and weighted, and both change how much the estimate wobbles from survey to survey. The honest margin carries that change through the design effect and the effective number of answers. Then comes the harder sentence: even the honest margin covers only the luck of the draw, and a report should say so.

LiveThree runs, three gaps between naive and honest4 min▶

Ottervale's plan, R4. Stratified by area, raked to the census on age, sex and area, weights trimmed at twice the mean. The lab prints 20.1% ± 5.4 as the honest margin against a naive ±5.2, with a design effect of 1.05 and an effective n of 214 from 226 answers. The gap is small because stratifying buys back some of the precision the weights spend.

The same plan without trimming, R3. Honest ±5.5 against naive ±5.2, design effect 1.10, effective n 206. Trimming (R4 capped 10 weights on its first pass) pulled the design effect from 1.10 to 1.05: a little precision bought back at a small cost in how far the weights can correct.

Clustered by street. Honest ±6.6 against naive ±6.0, design effect 1.23, effective n 181. Neighbours on one street resemble each other, so 223 answers carry the information of about 181. A report that prints ±6.0 here understates its own uncertainty.

The reporting rule. Print the honest margin, and next to it the design effect and the effective n, so a reader can see what the design cost. If the vendor can only give you the simple-random margin, the report says so.

LiveSay what the honest margin leaves out3 min▶

R4's own error line splits sample 1's miss into its parts: "Total error: -1.6 pts = coverage -0.3 · sampling +1.3 · nonresponse -0.6 · weighting -2.1". The margin speaks only to the sampling part. The coverage part is the residents the household address list never listed, such as people living in communal establishments; it is small here and no wider margin would reveal it. The nonresponse part is where the real exposure sits: if the people in poorer health answer less for reasons that age, sex and area do not capture, the weights cannot see it. The lab's R5 shows that world, where the same plan prints a confident interval that covers the truth on 5% of repeat surveys. One sentence in the report has to name that risk and its direction.

Self-studyWhere the design effect comes from3 min▶

Weighting alone inflates variance. A rule of thumb attributed to Kish (1992), reported here through Little and Vartivarian (2005), puts the increase at about the squared coefficient of variation of the weights, so the design effect from weighting is roughly 1 plus that square; the approximation holds best when the weighting variables are only weakly linked to the answer. Stratifying can pull the other way, which is why R2 on its own prints a design effect below 1. The lab does not use the rule of thumb: it computes the design effect from the actual weights and strata on every draw. The arithmetic of design effects and effective n is Analyst session 4's ground; Designs that spend the budget well works it through.

Real world

The profession separates margins from precision statements. AAPOR's Transparency Initiative asks member organisations to disclose the number of completed interviews and the precision of the results: a margin of sampling error where the sample was drawn at random, and a statement of precision for non-probability samples, where a classic margin has no footing. The disclosure item is about honesty of type, not only of size: the reader must be able to tell which kind of uncertainty statement they are looking at. (AAPOR, Transparency Initiative disclosure elements, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers the methods statement and what not to headline

A methods box a reader can audit, and four claims to keep out

5 min live

The methods box is where a careful reader goes first, so it should answer their questions in the order they ask them. The profession already publishes the list: AAPOR's twelve transparency items. Writing the box is mostly copying facts you already hold. The harder discipline is the headline, where four claims are tempting, quotable and wrong.

LiveThe twelve items, as Ottervale's methods box4 min▶

AAPOR's Transparency Initiative lists what a survey organisation discloses for every survey: the data collection strategy; the sponsor and who conducted it; the exact wording of the questions reported; the population; how the sample was generated and recruited (frame, probability or not, quotas, incentives); modes and languages; dates; the number of completes with a precision statement; the weighting, or a statement that none was used; data processing and quality checks; limitations; and the use of AI. Twelve items, and every one is a fact you already hold from the commissioning brief and the lab. The box costs a quarter of a page.

Name the response rate. "23% answered" is not yet a disclosure. AAPOR's Standard Definitions, now in their tenth edition (2023), define a family of response, cooperation, refusal and contact rates that treat ineligible and unreachable addresses differently. The lab's 23% is simply answers over addresses issued; a real report states which Standard Definitions rate it used, so two honest reports of the same fieldwork can be compared.

LiveFour claims that never belong in a headline4 min▶

1 · A subgroup from too few answers. R4's sample 1 prints "Answers per area: fewest 9 (area 1) · most 42 (area 8)". A share computed from nine people swings widely from one draw to the next. Your decision memo set a minimum before fieldwork; areas below it are merged or held back, and the report says which.

2 · A tight margin from a biased design. The lab's big preset issues 4,000 addresses with no weighting while older residents answer more. It prints ±2.8, and over 1,000 repeat surveys its bias is +5.2 points and the margin covers the truth 3% of the time. A small margin is a statement about chance, never about bias.

3 · "Representative" for an opt-in panel. Whether a weighted opt-in panel can stand for a population is a live argument, laid out with both sides in Leader session 2: AAPOR's 2010 online panels report advises against them for population values, while its 2013 task force places methods on a continuum and calls model-based approaches the most promising. The report does not settle it; it says what the sample was and lets the word "representative" go.

4 · A single draw presented as the design's record. Sample 1 of R4 is 20.1%, 1.6 points from the truth. Another draw of the same design lands elsewhere. What the design promises is its repeat line: "Over 1000 repeat surveys: bias +0.5 pts · spread (SD) 2.8 pts · honest margin covers the truth 95% · naive 94%". In a real survey you hold one draw and no truth; the report says it is one survey with its honest margin, and never that the design "gives" the number.

Self-studyWhen the honest answer to "how many?" is "we cannot say"2 min▶

Holding back a number is a finding, not a failure. "Results for area 1 are not shown separately because too few residents answered to give a reliable figure" is a sentence a council member can repeat. Printing area 1 anyway, with a footnote nobody reads, invites the one quote that will travel furthest. The rule from Leader session 3 applies at publication: suppress or merge below the minimum, and say you did.

Real world

One raw source, two readings. Wang, Rothschild, Goel and Gelman (2015) analysed Xbox opt-in polls from the 45 days before the 2012 US election: 750,148 interviews from 345,858 people, far from representative on age and sex. Raw, the sample was far from the electorate; after multilevel regression and poststratification, the estimates were in line with leading poll aggregators. The modelling made the difference, which is exactly why a methods box must say what was done. (Wang et al., International Journal of Forecasting 31(3), 2015, read at source.)

Part 3 · covers describing, not explaining

A share at one moment is not a cause

5 min live

Ottervale's survey says what share of adults report fair or worse health, the lab's stand-in for the council's question about who depends on the bus. That is a description: how many, at one time, with an honest margin. It does not say that the bus route keeps anyone well, or keeps anyone living in town. A headline that slides from the first to the second has changed the kind of claim, and no margin of error covers that slide.

LiveWords that turn a share into a cause3 min▶

Watch the verbs. "One adult in five reports fair or worse health" describes. "The bus route keeps one adult in five healthy" explains, and the survey never measured it. Neither did it measure what would happen if the route closed: nobody in the sample lived through that. Words to keep out of a descriptive headline: keeps, drives, because of, leads to, would. Words that stay honest: report, say, share, at the time of the survey.

The decision may still rest partly on a causal belief, that cutting the route would cut people off. The report's job is to keep that belief visible as a belief, separate from what the survey measured, so the council can weigh it with other evidence.

Self-studyWhere causal claims from observational data are taught1 min▶

When a council does want to know whether a service changes outcomes, it needs a design built for causes: a comparison group, a before and after, a natural experiment, and the assumptions each one rests on. That is a separate discipline, taught in Learn causal inference with Phoebe ↗. A survey can feed such a design; on its own it describes.

Real world

A number travels; its methods box stays behind. Pew Research Center reported in 2024 that 12% of adults under 30 in an online opt-in sample claimed a licence to operate a nuclear submarine, and that 20% of such a sample agreed the Holocaust is a myth against 3% in a probability survey. Lifted into a headline without the line that says "opt-in", either figure reads as a fact about young adults. The methods line is what tells a reader the number describes who answered, not the population. (Pew Research Center, "Online opt-in polls can produce misleading results, especially for young people and Hispanic adults", 2024, read at source.)

Homework

After the Leader track

30 min
  1. Take the last survey report your team published and check its margin: is it the honest one, with a design effect, or the simple-random one? Write which, and how you can tell.
  2. Score its methods box against the twelve transparency items. Count the items present, and write "not stated" for each missing one.
  3. Hunt its headline for the four claims: a thin subgroup, a tight margin offered as proof, "representative" for a non-random sample, a single result presented as a sure thing. Rewrite any you find.
  4. Find one causal verb in its summary or press line and rewrite the sentence as a description.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Ottervale's plan, R4, prints "20.1% ± 5.4 (honest margin) · naive ±5.2", with design effect 1.05. Which margin goes in the report?

The honest margin reflects the design that was actually run. Here the gap is small; on the clustered design it is ±6.6 against ±6.0. Printing the design effect lets a reader see what the design cost.

2 · Which of these may go in Ottervale's headline?

Nine answers cannot carry an area figure; the big unweighted design prints ±2.8 yet covers the truth 3% of the time; and whether an opt-in panel is "representative" is a contested question, not a headline.

3 · A draft press line reads "The bus route keeps one in five Ottervale adults healthy." What is wrong with it?

"Keeps healthy" is a causal claim. The survey measured how many, at one moment. A cause needs a design built for causes, not a bigger or better-weighted survey.
