Learn Survey Design & Sampling with Phoebe · Analyst session 3 of 6

Who you could have reached: the list before the sample

Every survey draws from a list, and nobody off the list can ever be asked. Before you choose a design or a sample size, you choose who is reachable at all: households at an address, people who joined an online panel, whoever walks down the high street on a Tuesday. Tonight you compare three such lists on real census rows standing in for Ottervale, a constructed town council, and then plan how many addresses to issue so the answer is precise enough to act on.

Part 0

Where we start

Analyst session 1 named coverage as the first gap between the town and the number; Analyst session 2 fixed the questions. Tonight is about the list itself. The Ottervale council wants to hear from every adult aged 16 and over, and it has three ways to reach them: a list of household addresses, an online opt-in panel, or a researcher with a clipboard on the high street. Each one decides, before a single question is asked, who can and cannot be in the answer. The council, its quotes and its decisions are constructed; the 20,000 adults are real rows from the Office for National Statistics' Census 2021 teaching sample, so the lab can measure exactly what each list leaves out.

Part 1 · covers population against frame

The list decides who can ever be asked

5 min live

The target population is who the decision is about: for Ottervale, every adult aged 16 and over. The sampling frame is the list you can actually draw from. Where the frame misses part of the population, that is undercoverage; where it holds people or places outside the population, that is overcoverage. The sample can only ever be a subset of the frame, so a gap in the list is a gap in every survey drawn from it, however carefully drawn.

LivePopulation, frame, and the gap between them3 min▶

Target population. Who the decision is about. For Ottervale's bus route, every adult aged 16 and over who lives in the town, including those who cannot get about easily, who are exactly the people the route matters most to.

Frame. The list you can draw a random sample from. An address list of private households is the usual choice for a town: every dwelling with a letterbox, each one with a known chance of being picked.

Undercoverage. Population members the frame cannot reach. A private-household list leaves out people living in communal establishments: care homes, halls of residence, hostels, prisons. National surveys make the same choice deliberately. The ONS Labour Force Survey covers people in private households and excludes most communal establishments, with two named exceptions: people in NHS accommodation, and students in halls, who are counted through their parents' household.

Why it matters here. Undercoverage moves the number only when the people left out differ on the thing you measure. Care home residents differ sharply on health and on bus use. On the bench, the address list holds 19,549 of the 20,000 adults and its coverage error is -0.3 points: small, because few adults are missed, but it points the expected way and no weighting of the people who were reachable can see the people who were not.

Self-studyOvercoverage and duplicates3 min▶

Overcoverage is the other direction: entries on the frame that are not in the population. An address list holds empty homes, holiday lets, businesses at residential addresses and homes where everyone is under 16. These are usually caught at contact ("nobody here is eligible") and treated as out of scope, not as refusals. Counting them as nonrespondents makes the response rate look worse than it is; counting them as eligible inflates the base the survey reports against.

Duplicates give some people more than one chance of selection: a family with two addresses, a panel member with two accounts, a respondent who answers a web link twice. A known duplicate can be corrected with a weight of one over the number of chances; an unknown one quietly over-represents whoever has more entries.

Addresses are not people. An address list samples households. To reach one adult per household you select within it (a random adult, or the one with the next birthday), and people in larger households then have a smaller chance of selection, which a weight must put back. Analyst session 5 returns to weights.

Real world

The national labour survey makes the same trade. The Office for National Statistics' Labour Force Survey samples private households, so most people in communal establishments are outside its coverage, with named exceptions for NHS accommodation and students in halls. It is a design choice, written down in the survey's quality and methodology information, so users know which population the published figures describe. Write Ottervale's equivalent sentence before you field anything. (ONS, Labour Force Survey QMI, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers panels and intercepts

Panels and intercepts reach whoever turned up

5 min live

Some frames are not lists at all. An opt-in online panel reaches only the people who chose to join one; a street intercept reaches whoever walks past the researcher at that hour. Neither gives each resident a known chance of selection, so there is no design to lean on: if joining or walking past is linked to the answer, the frame is biased before anyone is asked a question. On the bench the reach of both is simulated and labelled, so the lab can show what a linked reach does.

LiveWhy the panel and the intercept start below the truth3 min▶

The panel. In the lab, joining the opt-in panel is simulated by age, and adults in fair, bad or very bad health join less often. The panel ends up with 4,096 of the 20,000 adults, and its share in poorer health sits 9.6 points below the truth before anyone is sampled. The lab's panel preset also has older people answering more, which pushes the number back up by 3.8 points on average; the two errors partly cancel, and the result still misses by 5.9 points.

The intercept. A researcher on the high street on a weekday meets people by their daily activity: workers on a lunch break, shoppers, students. Reach is simulated by activity, and adults in poorer health are half as likely to be passing. The intercept frame holds 2,854 adults, and its coverage error is -4.9 points.

The pattern. In both cases the people the frame misses are the people the question is about. That is the worst kind of coverage error: it points the wrong way for the decision, and a bigger sample from the same frame only repeats it more confidently.

Self-studyWhat the panel's numbers can and cannot claim3 min▶

Without a known chance of selection, a panel's margin of error has no design to rest on. Any claim about the whole town relies on a model: an assumption that, within the groups you weight by, the people who joined answer like the people who did not. The lab's panel settings break that assumption on purpose, because joining is linked to health within each age group.

This is where professional opinion divides, and the disagreement is real. The 2010 AAPOR report on online panels is the cautious side. A later AAPOR task force (2013) argues that non-probability methods sit on a continuum of expected accuracy and that model-based approaches are the most promising, provided the assumptions are stated; Wang, Rothschild, Goel and Gelman (2015) brought an Xbox opt-in poll of 750,148 interviews into line with leading poll aggregators using multilevel regression and poststratification. Leader session 2 sets the two sides beside each other for a commissioning decision; tonight's job is only to see what the frame does before any model is applied.

Real world

The cautious side, in the profession's own words. AAPOR's 2010 report on online panels advises researchers to avoid nonprobability online panels when the goal is to estimate population values accurately. It found no accepted theoretical basis for projecting from them to a population, and reports that studies comparing them with benchmarks generally found them less accurate than probability methods. The report calls its own conclusions tentative. For the other side of the argument, see Leader session 2, Commissioning without regret. (Baker et al., AAPOR Report on Online Panels, Public Opinion Quarterly 74(4), 2010, read at source.)

Part 3 · covers sample size for a target margin

Size the sample backwards from the margin you need

5 min live

Once the frame is chosen, size is arithmetic worked backwards. Start from the margin the decision can tolerate, work out how many completed interviews that margin needs, inflate for the design, then divide by the share of people you expect to answer. The answer is how many addresses to issue, which is the number the fieldwork quote is built on.

LiveThree numbers, worked backwards3 min▶

Completes at simple random. For a proportion p and a margin m at 95%, completes = 1.96 squared × p(1 − p) ÷ m squared. With p = 22% and m = 3 points the planner prints 733; at ±5 points, 264. Halving the margin roughly quadruples the completes, which is why "a bit more precise" is never a small request.

Design effect. Stratifying, clustering and weighting change how much each interview is worth. A design effect of 1.2 means 1.2 completes do the work of one simple random complete, so ±3 points needs 879 completes and ±5 points needs 317. Where the 1.2 comes from, and how to make it smaller, is Analyst session 4.

Response rate. Issued = completes ÷ expected response rate. At 22% the planner prints 3,996 addresses for ±3 points and 1,441 for ±5. With no design effect, ±5 points needs 1,200 issued. If everyone answered, ±3 points would need only the 879.

This sizes a survey estimate: one share, with a margin around it. Sizing an A/B test is a different calculation, about the power to detect a difference between two groups, and it lives in Experimentation, power and sample size ↗.

Self-studyWhat the planner cannot buy2 min▶

The planner sizes precision, and only precision. No sample size buys back coverage error or nonresponse bias. Issue 3,996 addresses from a street intercept and you get a narrower interval around a number that sits below the truth for the same reason it did at 1,000. On the bench, the address list at 4,000 issued has repeat surveys that cover the truth 3% of the time, because the bias from who answers did not shrink while the interval did.

Two other choices hide inside the planner. The expected share: 22% is the bench's truth, rounded, and you would normally use a past survey or 50%, the worst case, which gives the largest sample. And the response rate: assume one you have evidence for, from a previous wave or a comparable survey. An optimistic rate is the most common reason a survey comes back short of its completes.

Real world

A response rate is bought with time and effort. In 2012 Pew Research Center ran its standard five-day phone survey, which reached a 9% response rate, beside a high-effort version fielded from January 5 to March 15 with incentives and refusal-conversion letters, which reached 22%. Ottervale's planner uses 22% as its expected rate; the constructed fieldwork quote should say what effort that rate assumes, because the same survey run for five days would return far fewer completes. (Pew Research Center, "Assessing the representativeness of public opinion surveys", 2012, read at source.)

Choose the frame on bias, then size it on precision. The panel and the intercept are cheaper per complete, and both start below the truth by more than the margin you are paying for. The address list starts close to the truth and leaves an error that weighting can work on. A sample size chosen first, on cost, would have bought a precise answer from the wrong list.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Ottervale's council samples from a list of private household addresses. Which residents can this survey never reach, however many addresses it issues?

Refusal is nonresponse, under-16s are outside the target population, and small areas are on the list. Only people off the frame have no chance of selection at all.

2 · On the bench, the opt-in panel's coverage error is -9.6 points. Why would a larger panel sample not fix it?

Coverage error is a property of the frame. More interviews from the same frame narrow the interval around the same shifted centre.

3 · The planner says ±3 points needs 879 completes with design effect 1.2, and 3,996 issued at 22% response. What should the fieldwork quote be built on?

Completes are what you need back; issued addresses are what you must send out to get them at the response rate you expect.
