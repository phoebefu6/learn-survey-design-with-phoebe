Part 1 · covers the ladder

Which choice moves which error

4 min live

Each rung changes one choice. The bias does not fall step by step. The address list removes the intercept's coverage error and exposes age-skewed nonresponse in the other direction; stratifying by area changes almost nothing; raking to census margins is the rung that brings the average estimate back to the truth. Then R5 takes it away again.

LiveWhy the rungs land where they do3 min▶

R0, street intercept. Whoever walks past on a weekday. Residents in poorer health are out less, so the list of people who could be stopped already holds too few of them: coverage -4.9 points before anyone is asked.

R1, address list. Every private household is on the list, so coverage falls to -0.3 (the care homes and halls it leaves out). But older residents answer more, and older residents report poorer health more often: nonresponse averages +5.6, and the bias flips to +5.2.

R2, stratified by area. The share in poorer health barely differs between areas, so fixing each area's share of the sample changes almost nothing: +5.3, 58%. Stratifying helps precision only when the strata differ on the answer.

R3, raking. Weights are adjusted until the respondents match the census on age, sex and area. Age is what drove the nonresponse, so the weights undo it: bias -0.4, and the honest margin covers the truth 94% of the time.

R4, trimming. Weights above twice the mean are capped. Precision improves a little and a little bias returns: +0.5, 95%. Part 3 reads that trade.

Self-studyWhat is real and what is simulated, on the widget3 min▶

Real: 20,000 adults drawn with a fixed seed from the 487,235 adults with a general-health answer in the Office for National Statistics' Census 2021 public microdata teaching sample, with their age band, sex, region, residence type, economic activity and general health. The truth, 21.7% in fair, bad or very bad health, is a fact about those rows.

Simulated, and stated on the widget: who joins the online panel, who walks past the intercept, who answers (the response propensities: 10% of 16 to 24 year olds up to 38% of those 65 and over, or 22% evenly), and the grouping of residents into streets of 20. Published response propensities by age and health for a town like Ottervale do not exist to borrow; the lab claims only the mechanism and its ordering, never that a real council would see these exact rates.

Real world

Raking, as the weighting literature describes it. Kalton and Flores-Cervantes describe raking as iterative proportional fitting: match the row totals to the population, then the column totals, then the rows again, until the weights settle. It uses only the margins, so it needs no cross-tabulated census cell, and it usually converges fast, though convergence is not guaranteed. The lab rakes on three margins for twenty rounds. (Kalton and Flores-Cervantes, "Weighting methods", Journal of Official Statistics, 2003, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers the anti-lever

The nonresponse no weight can see

4 min live

R5 keeps R4's design and weights exactly and changes one thing: who answers depends on the answer itself. Within every age band, residents in poorer health answer at about half the rate of their neighbours. The response rate barely moves, about 22% as before. The bias goes to -8.9 points and the honest margin covers the truth on 5% of repeats.

LiveWhy this is the whole response-rate debate in one rung3 min▶

R4 and R5 have the same response rate, about 22%. One is nearly unbiased after weighting; the other is off by 8.9 points. That is the mechanism Robert Groves set out in 2006: nonresponse can, but need not, create bias, and what decides it is how strongly answering is tied to the thing being measured (the propensity framing is reported here from a summary). The lab's "22% answer, evenly" setting gives -0.3; the outcome-linked one gives -8.9 at the same rate. The response rate alone cannot tell you which world you are in. Analyst session 5 teaches both sides of what follows from that.

Self-studyWhat you can do when weighting cannot see it3 min▶

Three moves, none of them free. Follow up a subsample of nonrespondents hard (by phone, by visit) and compare them with early answerers on the survey question itself. Find an auxiliary variable that tracks the answer and that you know for everyone, from the frame or a record match (here: a disability benefit flag would do it), and weight on that. Or say so in the report: name the plausible link and its direction, and widen the stated uncertainty. The lab cannot do the first two because the census rows carry nothing beyond what it already weights on; the third is Leader session 4.

Part 3 · covers the honest margin

The margin your design earned, not the one you hoped for

4 min live

Weights pull the estimate toward the truth and push the margin out. The lab computes the honest margin from the actual spread of weighted answers within strata and clusters, and prints it beside the naive one a calculator would give. Trimming the largest weights narrows it again, at a small price in bias. The design effect is the ratio of the two variances; the effective sample size is the respondents divided by it.

LiveReading the trade between R3 and R43 min▶

R3's weights vary enough to cost a design effect of 1.10 on sample 1: 226 answers carry the information of 206. R4 caps every weight above twice the mean and the design effect falls to 1.05, effective n 214. Over 1,000 repeats, R3's bias is -0.4 and R4's is +0.5: trimming gave back some of what raking fixed. Both cover the truth about 95% of the time. Trim when a handful of weights dominate and the bias cost is small; never trim to make a margin look tidy.

Self-studyThe weighting design effect, approximately2 min▶

Leslie Kish's rule of thumb for weighting is reported in a later paper by Little and Vartivarian: the variance grows by roughly the squared coefficient of variation of the weights, so the design effect from weighting is about one plus that, and the approximation is best when the weighting variable is only weakly tied to the answer. The lab does not use the shortcut. It computes the variance directly from the weighted answers, which is why its design effect can fall below 1 on a sample where stratification and the weights happen to help.

Real world

Precision bought with bias. The weighting literature describes bounding or trimming weights as a way to improve precision at some cost in bias reduction, which is exactly the R3 to R4 trade. (Kalton and Flores-Cervantes, Journal of Official Statistics, 2003, read at source.)

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · R4 and R5 have the same design, the same weights and about the same 22% response rate. Why is R5's bias -8.9 points?

Weights restore what the weighting variables can see. When answering tracks the outcome inside every weighting cell, the weights restore the cell's size, not its mix.

2 · Four thousand issued addresses with age-skewed answering and no weights give a margin of ±2.8. What does the repeat line show?

Sample size buys down sampling error only. With the same bias, a tighter interval misses the truth more often, not less.

3 · Trimming takes R3 to R4. Which pair of changes does the lab print?

Trimming buys precision with a little bias. Both rungs still cover the truth about 95% of the time.
