Every way a survey misses: six errors, one margin

A survey report prints one number and one margin of error, and most readers take the margin as the whole uncertainty. It is not. The margin describes what chance does when you draw a sample; it says nothing about who the list left out, who never answered, how the question was worded or how the weights were built. Tonight you meet the frame survey methodologists use for all of it, total survey error, and you run your first survey on real census rows standing in for Ottervale, a constructed town council with a bus route to defend.

Part 1 · covers the total survey error frame

From the town to the number, in five hand-offs

5 min live

A survey estimate is the end of a chain. You want to know something about a population; you can only reach the people on a list; you draw some of them; some of those answer; you weight the answers back toward the population. Each hand-off can lose people in a pattern, and a pattern that lines up with the answer moves the number. Survey methodologists call the sum of those gaps total survey error.

LiveThe errors, in the order a survey meets them3 min▶

Specification. The question measures something other than the concept the decision needs. Ottervale's council wants to know who depends on the bus; "do you use the bus?" counts the student who takes it twice a year.

Coverage. The list you draw from is not the population. An address list of private households leaves out people in care homes and halls; an online panel leaves out people who never joined one.

Sampling. You asked some, not all. This is the only error the textbook margin of error measures, and it shrinks as the sample grows.

Nonresponse. Some of the people you picked never answered. If the ones who answer differ from the ones who do not on the thing you are measuring, the number moves.

Measurement. The wording, the order, the mode or the interviewer moves the answer away from what the person would have said otherwise. Analyst session 2 is about nothing else.

Processing and adjustment. Coding, editing and weighting can introduce errors of their own, or fail to remove the ones above.

Self-studyWhere the frame comes from3 min▶

Paul Biemer's 2010 article in Public Opinion Quarterly sets total survey error out as a framework for getting the best data quality a budget allows, inside a wider idea of quality he calls fitness for use. The point is economic, not academic: every survey has a fixed budget, and the frame tells you where a pound buys the most accuracy. A bigger sample buys down sampling error only. If the largest error is coverage or nonresponse, a bigger sample buys a more confident wrong answer, which you will see on the bench in Analyst session 4. The error sources Biemer's introduction names (frame, sampling, interviewers, respondents, missing data, coding and editing) are reported here from a summary of the article, not read in full.

Real world

Fitness for use, not error for its own sake. Biemer's framework asks which errors to reduce and which only to measure, because no budget removes them all. A council survey that will decide a bus route needs its nonresponse bias measured; a quick pulse on library opening hours may only need it named. (Biemer, Public Opinion Quarterly, 2010, abstract read at source.)

Part 2 · covers what the margin of error covers

The margin describes chance, and only chance

5 min live

The usual margin of error is 1.96 times the standard error of a proportion from a simple random sample. It answers one question: if you drew another sample the same way from the same list, how far would the number move? It assumes the list is the population, everyone answered, and the question measured what you meant. When those fail, the interval sits around the wrong centre, and no amount of width puts it back.

LiveRead "95%" as a promise about a process3 min▶

A 95% interval is a statement about the method: drawn many times, 95% of such intervals would contain the true value, if the assumptions held. The lab can test that promise because it knows the truth. Draw 1,000 surveys of the same design, build each one's interval, count how many contain the truth. With age-skewed nonresponse and no weighting (the lab's R1), the count is 58%. With answering spread evenly (the "uniform" setting), it is 94%. Same sample size, same width; the difference is all bias.

The mathematics of the interval itself (standard errors, the central limit theorem, why 1.96) lives in Statistics, confidence intervals ↗. Here the interval is taken as given, and the question is what it leaves out.

Self-studyMean squared error: bias and spread in one number3 min▶

Survey methodologists summarise accuracy as mean squared error: the variance of the estimate plus its bias squared. Variance falls as the sample grows; bias does not. That is why a large biased survey can be less accurate than a small unbiased one, and why the total survey error frame insists on budgeting for every term. The lab prints both parts on its repeat line: "bias" and "spread (SD)".

Real world

Two phone surveys, 9% and 22% response. In 2012 Pew Research Center ran its standard five-day survey (9% response) beside a high-effort version fielded over ten weeks with incentives and refusal conversion (22% response). Of 40 comparisons, 28 differed by 2 points or less. A low response rate did not, on most measures, mean a large bias. Analyst session 5 teaches why that is real and why it is not the whole story. (Pew Research Center, "Assessing the representativeness of public opinion surveys", 2012, read at source.)

Part 3 · covers the error budget

An error budget names where each point came from

5 min live

An error budget is a table with one row per error source and two columns: how big you expect it to be, and what the plan spends on it. The lab fills the expected column for you by splitting each run's error into coverage, sampling, nonresponse and weighting. On Ottervale's first two designs the split tells two different stories: the street intercept loses the people in poorer health before anyone is asked; the address list reaches them, and then the young stop answering.

LiveHow the lab splits one run's error3 min▶

Because the lab knows every one of its 20,000 residents, it can measure four averages at each hand-off: the whole population (the truth), the frame, the issued sample and the respondents, then the weighted estimate. Coverage is frame minus truth. Sampling is issued sample minus frame. Nonresponse is respondents minus issued sample. Weighting is the estimate minus the respondents. The four add up to the total error, exactly, on every run.

The truth is the share of adults whose general health is fair, bad or very bad: 21.7% of the 20,000. It stands in for the council's real question, how many residents depend on the bus because getting about is hard. The stand-in is labelled on the widget. What is simulated is who joins a panel, who walks past an intercept and who answers; those propensities are printed on the widget and in the session where each one matters.

Self-studyMeasurement and processing are not on the bench2 min▶

The lab asks every resident the census question exactly as the census asked it, so its measurement error is zero by construction, and it does no coding. Real surveys are not so lucky. Analyst session 2 covers measurement with published wording and order experiments; your error budget should still carry a row for both, with a plan (pretesting, a coding check) instead of a number.

Never trust a single draw. R1's sample 1 is off by 4.0 points while the design's bias is 5.2; another sample could be off by 1 or by 9. The design, judged over many draws, is the thing you are choosing. That is why the lab always prints the repeat line beside the single estimate.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A report prints "45% ± 3 points". Which error does the ± 3 describe?

The textbook margin assumes the list is the population, everyone answered and the question measured what was meant. It describes chance only.

2 · On the lab, R1's 1,000 repeat surveys show bias +5.2 points and the honest margin covers the truth 58% of the time. Why not 95%?

Coverage of an interval falls when the estimate is biased. With answering spread evenly the same design covers the truth 94% of the time.

3 · Moving from the street intercept (R0) to the address list (R1) cuts coverage error from -4.9 to -0.3 points. Why does total bias not fall?

Errors come from different hand-offs and can point in opposite directions. Removing one exposes the next; an error budget keeps them all in view.
